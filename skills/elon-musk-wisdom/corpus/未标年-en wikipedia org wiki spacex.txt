**Space Exploration Technologies Corp.**, [doing business as](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trade_name "Trade name") **SpaceX**, is an American [aerospace manufacturer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace_manufacturer "Aerospace manufacturer") and provider of [space transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_transportation "Space transportation"), [telecommunications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_industry "Telecommunications industry"), and [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") services, headquartered at the [Starbase development site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starbase "SpaceX Starbase") in [Starbase, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starbase,_Texas "Starbase, Texas").[\[7\]](#cite_note-7)[\[8\]](#cite_note-8) The company operates three primary segments: [Space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_technology "Space technology"), which develops and flies the [Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9 "Falcon 9"), [Falcon Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy "Falcon Heavy"), and [Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship "SpaceX Starship") launch vehicles, as well as the [Dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Dragon "SpaceX Dragon") spacecraft, and conducts more orbital launches annually than any other launch provider (including national programs);[\[9\]](#cite_note-9)[\[10\]](#cite_note-10) Connectivity, which operates the [Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink "Starlink") [satellite constellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_constellation "Satellite constellation") and satellite-based high-speed broadband internet service; and [AI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence"), conducted through its wholly owned subsidiary [SpaceXAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceXAI "SpaceXAI"), which develops the [Grok](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grok_\(chatbot\) "Grok (chatbot)") family of [large language models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model "Large language model") (LLMs) and related products, operates the social network [X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X_\(social_network\) "X (social network)") (formerly Twitter), and builds and runs large-scale [data centers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colossus_\(data_center\) "Colossus (data center)").[\[11\]](#cite_note-11)

The company is credited with advances in [rocket propulsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_propulsion "Rocket propulsion"), [reusable launch vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reusable_launch_vehicle "Reusable launch vehicle"), [human spaceflight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_spaceflight "Human spaceflight"), and [satellite constellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_constellation "Satellite constellation") technology. The company's largest customers include NASA, the [United States Space Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Space_Force "United States Space Force"), and the [National Reconnaissance Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Reconnaissance_Office "National Reconnaissance Office"). [Elon Musk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elon_Musk "Elon Musk") owns 39% of the outstanding shares of SpaceX (plus a number of vested [options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Option_\(finance\) "Option (finance)") and unvested [restricted shares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restricted_stock "Restricted stock")) and controls 84% of the voting power via his [super-voting stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super-voting_stock "Super-voting stock"). SpaceX was founded in 2002 by Elon Musk with the goal of reducing spaceflight costs, improving the reliability of access to [outer space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_space "Outer space") for ordinary humans, and [colonizing Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_ambition_of_colonizing_Mars "SpaceX ambition of colonizing Mars"). In 2008, after three failed attempts between 2006 and 2008 that almost pushed the company to bankruptcy, SpaceX successfully launched the [Falcon 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_1 "Falcon 1") into orbit, becoming the first private company to develop and launch a [liquid-fueled rocket](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid-propellant_rocket "Liquid-propellant rocket") to orbit. In 2010, the company launched the [Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9 "Falcon 9") launch vehicle and the [Dragon 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_1 "Dragon 1") spacecraft to fulfill [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA")'s [Commercial Orbital Transportation Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Orbital_Transportation_Services "Commercial Orbital Transportation Services") (COTS) contracts for cargo deliveries to the [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station "International Space Station") (ISS). In 2012, SpaceX began flying [Commercial Resupply Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Resupply_Services "Commercial Resupply Services") missions to the ISS, becoming the first private company to successfully dock with the station, and started developing technologies to make the Falcon 9 [first stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multistage_rocket "Multistage rocket") reusable. In 2015, [Falcon 9 flight 20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_flight_20 "Falcon 9 flight 20") was the first successful landing of an orbital-class rocket's first stage and the company's [SES-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SES-10 "SES-10") completed the first reflight of an orbital-class booster in 2017. After a decade of development, the [Falcon Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy "Falcon Heavy"), which consists of three Falcon 9-derived boosters, [made its maiden flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy_test_flight "Falcon Heavy test flight") in 2018. As of May 2026, Falcon 9 launches averaged approximately three missions per week, and Falcon boosters had completed [nearly 650 landings and reflights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Falcon_9_and_Falcon_Heavy_launches "List of Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy launches").

In 2019, the first operational satellite of the Starlink internet [satellite constellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_constellation "Satellite constellation") came online, [diversifying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diversification_\(marketing_strategy\) "Diversification (marketing strategy)") the revenue of the company. Starlink has generated the bulk of SpaceX's income and led to its [Starshield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starshield "SpaceX Starshield") military counterpart. In 2020, SpaceX began to operate its [Dragon 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_2 "Dragon 2") capsules for NASA and private entity [crewed missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_spaceflight "Human spaceflight"). Around this time, SpaceX began building test prototypes for [SpaceX Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship "SpaceX Starship"), the largest launch vehicle in history. SpaceX plans to adapt it as a [human lander for lunar missions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_HLS "Starship HLS") under NASA's [Artemis program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_program "Artemis program").[\[12\]](#cite_note-lander-12) In 2026, SpaceX acquired Musk's artificial intelligence company xAI (subsequently operated as the wholly owned subsidiary [SpaceXAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceXAI "SpaceXAI")), and announced a joint venture with [Tesla, Inc.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla,_Inc. "Tesla, Inc.") to build [Terafab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terafab "Terafab"), a large plant for [semiconductor device fabrication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_device_fabrication "Semiconductor device fabrication"). The [initial public offering of SpaceX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_public_offering_of_SpaceX "Initial public offering of SpaceX") on June 12, 2026, which raised $86 billion,[\[13\]](#cite_note-13) was the largest IPO in history and briefly made Musk the first US-dollar [trillionaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trillionaire "Trillionaire").[\[14\]](#cite_note-14)[\[15\]](#cite_note-15)

## History

### 2001–2002: Founding

In early 2001, [Elon Musk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elon_Musk "Elon Musk") met [Robert Zubrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Zubrin "Robert Zubrin") and donated $100,000 to his [Mars Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars_Society "Mars Society") and briefly joined its board of directors.[\[16\]](#cite_note-16): 30–31  In August 2001, he gave a [plenary talk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plenary_talk "Plenary talk") at their fourth convention where he announced _Mars Oasis_, a project to land a [greenhouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenhouse "Greenhouse") and grow plants on Mars.[\[17\]](#cite_note-17)[\[18\]](#cite_note-18)

Musk initially attempted to acquire a [Dnepr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dnepr_\(rocket\) "Dnepr (rocket)") launch vehicle for the project through Russian contacts provided by [Jim Cantrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Cantrell "Jim Cantrell"); however, he was unsuccessful.[\[19\]](#cite_note-bankruptcy-19)

Musk returned with his team to Moscow, this time bringing [Michael D. Griffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_D._Griffin "Michael D. Griffin"), who later became the 11th [Administrator of NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Administrator_of_NASA "Administrator of NASA"), but found the Russians increasingly unreceptive.[\[20\]](#cite_note-Changing-20)[\[21\]](#cite_note-dream-21) On the flight home, Musk stated that he could start a company to build the affordable rockets the project required.[\[21\]](#cite_note-dream-21) By applying [vertical integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_integration "Vertical integration"),[\[20\]](#cite_note-Changing-20) using inexpensive [commercial off-the-shelf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_off-the-shelf "Commercial off-the-shelf") components when possible,[\[21\]](#cite_note-dream-21) and adopting the [modular approach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_Approach_to_Software_Construction_Operation_and_Test "Modular Approach to Software Construction Operation and Test") of modern software engineering, Musk believed SpaceX could significantly cut launch costs.[\[21\]](#cite_note-dream-21)

In early 2002, Elon Musk started to look for staff for his company, soon to be named SpaceX. Musk approached five people for the initial positions, including Griffin, who declined the position of Chief Engineer,[\[22\]](#cite_note-Liftoff2021-22): 11  [Jim Cantrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Cantrell "Jim Cantrell") and John Garvey (Cantrell and Garvey later founded the company [Vector Launch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_Launch "Vector Launch")), rocket engineer [Tom Mueller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Mueller "Tom Mueller"), and Chris Thompson.[\[22\]](#cite_note-Liftoff2021-22)[\[23\]](#cite_note-23) SpaceX was first headquartered in a warehouse in [El Segundo, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Segundo,_California "El Segundo, California"). Early SpaceX employees, such as Tom Mueller (CTO), [Gwynne Shotwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwynne_Shotwell "Gwynne Shotwell") (COO), and Chris Thompson (VP of Operations), were previously employed at [TRW](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRW_Inc. "TRW Inc.") and [Boeing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing "Boeing"). Musk personally interviewed and approved all of SpaceX's early employees.[\[22\]](#cite_note-Liftoff2021-22): 22 

### 2003–2010: Falcon 1, COTS, and Falcon 9

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c8/Falcon_1_Flight_4_liftoff.jpg/250px-Falcon_1_Flight_4_liftoff.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Falcon_1_Flight_4_liftoff.jpg)

The first successful [Falcon 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_1 "Falcon 1") launch in September 2008

SpaceX developed its first [orbital launch vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_launch_vehicle "Orbital launch vehicle"), the [Falcon 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_1 "Falcon 1"), with internal funding.[\[24\]](#cite_note-24)[\[25\]](#cite_note-Mission-25) The Falcon 1 was an [expendable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expendable_launch_system "Expendable launch system") [two-stage-to-orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-stage-to-orbit "Two-stage-to-orbit") [small-lift launch vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small-lift_launch_vehicle "Small-lift launch vehicle"). The total development cost of Falcon 1 was approximately $90 million[\[26\]](#cite_note-26) to $100 million.[\[22\]](#cite_note-Liftoff2021-22): 215  The Falcon rocket series was named after _[Star Wars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Wars "Star Wars")_'s _[Millennium Falcon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millennium_Falcon "Millennium Falcon")_ fictional spacecraft.[\[19\]](#cite_note-bankruptcy-19)

In 2004, SpaceX protested against NASA to the [Government Accountability Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Accountability_Office "Government Accountability Office") (GAO) because of a sole-source contract awarded to [Kistler Aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kistler_Aerospace "Kistler Aerospace"). Before the GAO could respond, NASA withdrew the contract, and formed the [Commercial Orbital Transportation Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Orbital_Transportation_Services "Commercial Orbital Transportation Services") (COTS) program.[\[22\]](#cite_note-Liftoff2021-22): 109–110 [\[27\]](#cite_note-27) In 2005, SpaceX announced plans to pursue a human-rated commercial space program through the end of the decade, a program that would later become the [Dragon spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_spacecraft "Dragon spacecraft").[\[28\]](#cite_note-28)

SpaceX originally intended to follow its light Falcon 1 launch vehicle with an intermediate capacity vehicle, the [Falcon 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_5 "Falcon 5").[\[29\]](#cite_note-29) The company instead decided in 2005 to proceed with the development of the [Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9 "Falcon 9"), a [medium-lift launch vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium-lift_launch_vehicle "Medium-lift launch vehicle") intended to be [reusable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reusable_launch_vehicle "Reusable launch vehicle"). Development of the Falcon 9 was accelerated by [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA"), which committed to purchasing several commercial flights if specific capabilities were demonstrated. In 2006, the company was selected by NASA and awarded $396 million to provide crew and cargo resupply demonstration contracts to the International Space Station (ISS) under the COTS program.[\[30\]](#cite_note-30) The contract provided development funding for the [Dragon spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_spacecraft "Dragon spacecraft"), Falcon 9, and demonstration launches of Falcon 9 with Dragon.[\[31\]](#cite_note-Lindenmoyer_quoted-31)

The first two Falcon 1 launches were purchased by the [United States Department of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense "United States Department of Defense") under the [DARPA Falcon Project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DARPA_Falcon_Project "DARPA Falcon Project") which evaluated new U.S. launch vehicles suitable for use in hypersonic missile delivery for [Prompt Global Strike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prompt_Global_Strike "Prompt Global Strike").[\[25\]](#cite_note-Mission-25)[\[32\]](#cite_note-32)[\[33\]](#cite_note-33) The first three launches of the rocket, between 2006 and 2008, all resulted in failures, which almost ended the company. Financing for Tesla Motors had failed, as well,[\[34\]](#cite_note-Levin2022-34) and consequently [Tesla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla,_Inc. "Tesla, Inc."), [SolarCity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SolarCity "SolarCity"), and Musk personally were all nearly bankrupt at the same time.[\[19\]](#cite_note-bankruptcy-19)[\[22\]](#cite_note-Liftoff2021-22): 178–182  Musk was reportedly "waking from nightmares, screaming and in physical pain" because of the stress.[\[22\]](#cite_note-Liftoff2021-22): 216 

The financial situation started to turn around with the first successful launch achieved on [the fourth attempt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_1#Fourth_flight "Falcon 1") on September 28, 2008.[\[35\]](#cite_note-35) Musk split his remaining $30 million between SpaceX and Tesla, and NASA awarded SpaceX a $1.6 billion [Commercial Resupply Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Resupply_Services "Commercial Resupply Services") (CRS) contract in December, thus financially saving the company.[\[22\]](#cite_note-Liftoff2021-22): 217–221  Based on these factors and the further business operations they enabled, the Falcon 1 was soon retired following its [second successful, and fifth total, launch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_1#Fifth_flight "Falcon 1") in July 2009.[\[36\]](#cite_note-36) This allowed SpaceX to focus company resources on the development of a larger orbital rocket, the Falcon 9.[\[37\]](#cite_note-37) [Gwynne Shotwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwynne_Shotwell "Gwynne Shotwell") was also promoted to company president at the time, for her role in successfully negotiating the CRS contract with the [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA") Associate Administrator [Bill Gerstenmaier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Gerstenmaier "Bill Gerstenmaier").[\[38\]](#cite_note-38)[\[22\]](#cite_note-Liftoff2021-22): 222 

As part of the COTS contract, the Falcon 9 launched for the first time in June 2010 with the [Dragon Spacecraft Qualification Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_Spacecraft_Qualification_Unit "Dragon Spacecraft Qualification Unit"), using a mockup of the Dragon spacecraft.

The first operational Dragon spacecraft was launched in December 2010 aboard [COTS Demo Flight 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COTS_Demo_Flight_1 "COTS Demo Flight 1"), the Falcon 9's second flight, and safely returned to Earth after two orbits, completing all its mission objectives.[\[39\]](#cite_note-splash-39) By December 2010, the SpaceX production line was manufacturing one Falcon 9 and Dragon every three months.[\[40\]](#cite_note-spacedotcom20101209-40)

### 2011–2018: Falcon 9, Dragon, and NASA contracts

Video of the first launch of Falcon 9

In April 2011, as part of its second-round [Commercial Crew Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Crew_Development "Commercial Crew Development") (CCDev) program, NASA issued a $75 million contract for SpaceX to develop an integrated [launch escape system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_escape_system "Launch escape system") for Dragon in preparation for human-rating it as a crew transport vehicle to the ISS.[\[41\]](#cite_note-sdc20110418-41) NASA awarded SpaceX a fixed-price [Space Act Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Act_Agreement "Space Act Agreement") (SAA) to produce a detailed design of the crew transportation system in August 2012.[\[42\]](#cite_note-Koenigsmann_2018-42)

In early 2012, approximately two-thirds of SpaceX stock was owned by Musk[\[43\]](#cite_note-43) and his seventy million shares were then estimated to be worth $875 million on [private markets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_equity "Private equity"),[\[44\]](#cite_note-IPO-44) valuing SpaceX at $1.3 billion.[\[45\]](#cite_note-cnbc20120427-45) In May 2012, with the [Dragon C2+](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_C2+ "Dragon C2+") launch, Dragon became the first commercial spacecraft to deliver cargo to the [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station "International Space Station").[\[46\]](#cite_note-cbcnews-46) After the flight, the company private equity valuation nearly doubled to $2.4 billion or $20/share.[\[47\]](#cite_note-47)[\[48\]](#cite_note-48) By that time, SpaceX had operated on total funding of approximately $1 billion over its first decade of operation. Of this, private equity provided approximately $200 million, with Musk investing approximately $100 million and other investors having put in about $100 million.[\[49\]](#cite_note-49)

SpaceX's active reusability test program began in late 2012 with testing low-altitude, low-speed aspects of the landing technology.[\[50\]](#cite_note-qz_2017-50) The [Falcon 9 prototypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_prototypes "Falcon 9 prototypes") performed vertical takeoffs and landings ([VTOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VTOL "VTOL")). High-velocity, high-altitude tests of the [booster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Booster_\(rocketry\) "Booster (rocketry)") atmospheric return technology began in late 2013.[\[50\]](#cite_note-qz_2017-50)

#### Commercial launches and rapid growth

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1d/Launch_of_Falcon_9_carrying_ORBCOMM_OG2-M1_%2816601442698%29.jpg/250px-Launch_of_Falcon_9_carrying_ORBCOMM_OG2-M1_%2816601442698%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Launch_of_Falcon_9_carrying_ORBCOMM_OG2-M1_\(16601442698\).jpg)

Launch of Falcon 9 carrying [ORBCOMM OG2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbcomm-OG2 "Orbcomm-OG2")\-M1, July 2014

SpaceX launched the first commercial mission for a private customer in 2013. In 2014, SpaceX won nine contracts out of the 20 that were openly competed worldwide.[\[51\]](#cite_note-sn20150112-51) That year [Arianespace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arianespace "Arianespace") requested that European governments provide additional [subsidies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsidies "Subsidies") to face the competition from SpaceX.[\[52\]](#cite_note-aw20140211-52)[\[53\]](#cite_note-sn20140414-53) Beginning in 2014, SpaceX capabilities and pricing also began to affect the market for launch of U.S. military payloads, which for nearly a decade had been dominated by the large U.S. launch provider [United Launch Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Launch_Alliance "United Launch Alliance") (ULA).[\[54\]](#cite_note-lat20141125-54) The monopoly had allowed launch costs by the U.S. provider to rise to over $400 million over the years.[\[55\]](#cite_note-55) In September 2014, NASA's Director of Commercial Spaceflight, Kevin Crigler, awarded SpaceX the Commercial Crew Transportation Capability (CCtCap) contract to finalize the development of the Crew Transportation System. The contract included several technical and certification milestones, an uncrewed flight test, a crewed flight test, and six operational missions after certification.[\[42\]](#cite_note-Koenigsmann_2018-42)

In January 2015, SpaceX raised $1 billion in funding from Google and [Fidelity Investments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fidelity_Investments "Fidelity Investments"), in exchange for 8.33% of the company, establishing the company valuation at approximately $12 billion.[\[56\]](#cite_note-56)[\[57\]](#cite_note-57) The same month SpaceX announced the development of a new satellite constellation, called [Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink "Starlink"), to provide global broadband internet service with 4,000 satellites.[\[58\]](#cite_note-58)

The Falcon 9 had its first major failure in late June 2015, when the seventh ISS resupply mission, [CRS-7](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRS-7 "CRS-7") exploded two minutes into the flight. The problem was traced to a failed two-foot-long steel strut that held a [helium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helium "Helium") [pressure vessel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_vessel "Pressure vessel"), which broke free due to the force of [acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acceleration "Acceleration"). This caused a breach and allowed high-pressure helium to escape into the low-pressure [propellant tank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propellant_tank "Propellant tank"), causing the failure.[\[59\]](#cite_note-59)

#### Reusability milestones

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/54/CRS-8_%2826239020092%29.jpg/250px-CRS-8_%2826239020092%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:CRS-8_\(26239020092\).jpg)

Falcon 9 first stage on an [autonomous spaceport drone ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship "Autonomous spaceport drone ship") (ASDS) [barge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barge "Barge") after the first successful landing at sea, [SpaceX CRS-8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_CRS-8 "SpaceX CRS-8") mission

SpaceX first achieved a successful landing and recovery of a first stage in December 2015 with [Falcon 9 Flight 20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Flight_20 "Falcon 9 Flight 20").[\[60\]](#cite_note-60) In April 2016, the company achieved the first successful landing on the autonomous spaceport drone ship (ASDS) _[Of Course I Still Love You](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Of_Course_I_Still_Love_You "Of Course I Still Love You")_ in the Atlantic Ocean.[\[61\]](#cite_note-61) By October 2016, following the successful landings, SpaceX indicated they were offering their customers a 10% price discount if they choose to fly their payload on a reused Falcon 9 first stage.[\[62\]](#cite_note-sn20161005-62)

A second major rocket failure happened in early September 2016, when a Falcon 9 exploded during a propellant fill operation for a standard pre-launch [static fire test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_fire_test "Static fire test"). The payload, the [AMOS-6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMOS-6 "AMOS-6") communications satellite valued at $200 million, was destroyed.[\[63\]](#cite_note-63) The explosion was caused by the [liquid oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen "Liquid oxygen") that is used as propellant turning so cold that it solidified and ignited with [carbon composite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_overwrapped_pressure_vessel "Composite overwrapped pressure vessel") helium vessels.[\[64\]](#cite_note-verge-sept-explod-cause-64) Though not considered an unsuccessful flight, the rocket explosion sent the company into a four-month launch hiatus while it worked out what went wrong. SpaceX returned to flight in January 2017.[\[65\]](#cite_note-plarson-65)

In March 2017, SpaceX launched a returned Falcon 9 for the [SES-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SES-10 "SES-10") satellite. This was the first time a re-launch of a payload-carrying orbital rocket went back to space.[\[66\]](#cite_note-Davenport2017-66) The first stage was recovered again, also making it the first landing of a reused orbital class rocket.[\[67\]](#cite_note-67)

#### Leading global commercial launch provider

In 2017, SpaceX formed a subsidiary, [The Boring Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Boring_Company "The Boring Company"), and began work to construct a short test tunnel on land adjacent to the SpaceX headquarters and manufacturing facility, using a small number of SpaceX employees,[\[68\]](#cite_note-lat20171121-68) which was completed in May 2018,[\[69\]](#cite_note-69) and opened to the public in December 2018.[\[70\]](#cite_note-wsj20181217-70) During 2018, The Boring Company was [spun out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spun_out "Spun out") into a separate [corporate entity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_entity "Corporate entity") with 6% of the equity going to SpaceX, less than 10% to early employees, and the remainder of the equity to Elon Musk.[\[70\]](#cite_note-wsj20181217-70)

In July 2017, the company raised $350 million at a valuation of $21 billion.[\[71\]](#cite_note-71) In 2017, SpaceX achieved a 45% global market share for awarded commercial launch contracts.[\[72\]](#cite_note-72)

By March 2018, SpaceX had more than 100 launches on its manifest representing about $12 billion in contract revenue. The contracts included both commercial and [government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_government_of_the_United_States "Federal government of the United States") (NASA/DOD) customers. This made SpaceX the leading global commercial launch provider measured by manifested launches.[\[73\]](#cite_note-73)

### 2019–2020: Raising capital, Starlink launch, and crewed launches

In January 2019, SpaceX announced it would lay off 10% of its workforce to help finance the [Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink "Starlink") and [Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship "SpaceX Starship") projects.[\[74\]](#cite_note-74) The purpose of the Starship vehicle is to enable large-scale transit of humans and cargo to the Moon, Mars, and beyond.[\[75\]](#cite_note-75) SpaceX's Starship is the largest and most powerful rocket ever flown, with a planned payload capacity of 100+ tons.[\[76\]](#cite_note-starship-flight-3-76)[\[77\]](#cite_note-starship-press-kit-77) Construction of initial prototypes and tests for Starship started in early 2019 in Florida and Texas. All Starship construction and testing moved to the new [SpaceX South Texas launch site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_South_Texas_launch_site "SpaceX South Texas launch site") later that year.

In 2019 SpaceX raised $1.33 billion across three funding rounds.[\[78\]](#cite_note-cnbc20200221-78) By May 2019, the valuation of SpaceX had risen to $33.3 billion[\[79\]](#cite_note-rises-79) and reached $36 billion by March 2020.[\[80\]](#cite_note-80)

In May 2019, SpaceX launched the first large batch of 60 Starlink satellites, beginning to deploy what would become the world's largest commercial satellite constellation the following year.[\[81\]](#cite_note-81) On August 19, 2020, after a $1.9 billion raise, one of the largest by any private company, valuation increased to $46 billion.[\[82\]](#cite_note-82)[\[83\]](#cite_note-83)[\[84\]](#cite_note-cnbc20210216-84)

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/6/68/KSC-20200523-PH-KLS03_0011.jpg/250px-KSC-20200523-PH-KLS03_0011.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:KSC-20200523-PH-KLS03_0011.jpg)

NASA astronauts Douglas Hurley (left) and Robert Behnken (right) wearing custom-fit spacesuits designed by SpaceX.

In May 2020, SpaceX successfully launched two NASA astronauts ([Doug Hurley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doug_Hurley "Doug Hurley") and [Bob Behnken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Behnken "Bob Behnken")) into orbit on a [Crew Dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon "Crew Dragon") spacecraft during [Crew Dragon Demo-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon_Demo-2 "Crew Dragon Demo-2"), making SpaceX the first private company to send astronauts to the International Space Station and marking the first crewed orbital launch from American soil in 9 years.[\[85\]](#cite_note-Lifts-85)[\[86\]](#cite_note-86) The mission launched from [Kennedy Space Center Launch Complex 39A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Space_Center_Launch_Complex_39A "Kennedy Space Center Launch Complex 39A") (LC-39A) in Florida.[\[87\]](#cite_note-87)

### 2021–present

In February 2021, SpaceX raised an additional $1.61 billion from 99 investors[\[88\]](#cite_note-sn20210415-88) at a per share value of approximately $420,[\[84\]](#cite_note-cnbc20210216-84) lifting the valuation to ~$74 billion. By 2021, SpaceX had raised more than $6 billion. Most of the capital raised since 2019 was used to support the Starlink satellite constellation and the development and manufacture of Starship.[\[88\]](#cite_note-sn20210415-88) By October 2021, the valuation grew to $100.3 billion.[\[89\]](#cite_note-89)

On April 16, 2021, Starship HLS won a NASA contract for the [Artemis program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artemis_program "Artemis program").[\[12\]](#cite_note-lander-12) In 2021, SpaceX entered into agreements with [Google Cloud Platform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Cloud_Platform "Google Cloud Platform") and [Microsoft Azure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Azure "Microsoft Azure") to provide on-ground computer and networking services for [Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink "Starlink").[\[90\]](#cite_note-90) A funding round in 2022 valued SpaceX at $127 billion.[\[91\]](#cite_note-raises-91)

In July 2021, SpaceX unveiled another drone ship named _[A Shortfall of Gravitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Shortfall_of_Gravitas "A Shortfall of Gravitas")_, first landing [CRS-23](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRS-23 "CRS-23") on August 29, 2021.[\[92\]](#cite_note-92) Within the first 130 days of 2022, SpaceX launched 18 rockets and returned two crews. On December 13, 2021, Musk announced that the company was starting a [carbon dioxide removal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide_removal "Carbon dioxide removal") program that would [convert captured carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_capture_and_utilization "Carbon capture and utilization") into [rocket fuel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_fuel "Rocket fuel"),[\[93\]](#cite_note-93)[\[94\]](#cite_note-94) after he announced a $100 million donation to the [X Prize Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X_Prize_Foundation "X Prize Foundation") the previous February to fund a contest to develop the best [carbon capture technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_air_capture "Direct air capture").[\[95\]](#cite_note-95)[\[96\]](#cite_note-96)

In 2022, most SpaceX launches focused on Starlink, a consumer internet business that sends batches of internet-beaming satellites and now has over 6,000 satellites in orbit.[\[97\]](#cite_note-sn2674441-97) SpaceX launched a rocket approximately every six days in 2022, with 61 launches in total, the most launches ever of a single vehicle type in a single year.[\[98\]](#cite_note-Wall_2023-98) All but one (a Falcon Heavy in November) was a Falcon 9.[\[98\]](#cite_note-Wall_2023-98) In August 2022, the [European Space Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Space_Agency "European Space Agency") (ESA) began discussions with SpaceX to substitute for Russian launches, amid the [Russian invasion of Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_invasion_of_Ukraine "Russian invasion of Ukraine").[\[99\]](#cite_note-99)

In November 2023, SpaceX announced it would acquire its parachute supplier [Pioneer Aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pioneer_Aerospace "Pioneer Aerospace") out of bankruptcy for $2.2 million.[\[100\]](#cite_note-100)[\[101\]](#cite_note-101)

In 2024, SpaceX moved its headquarters from [Hawthorne, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawthorne,_California "Hawthorne, California"), to [SpaceX Starbase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starbase "SpaceX Starbase") in [Brownsville, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownsville,_Texas "Brownsville, Texas"), ostensibly because California increasingly became a difficult place to operate.[\[102\]](#cite_note-102) The Hawthorne facility continues to support the company's Falcon launch business.[\[103\]](#cite_note-103)

SpaceX's 2024 [Polaris Dawn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaris_Dawn "Polaris Dawn") mission featured the first-ever private spacewalk.[\[104\]](#cite_note-104)

By July 2025, as part of a $5 billion equity raise, SpaceX agreed to invest $2 billion in [xAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XAI_\(company\) "XAI (company)").[\[105\]](#cite_note-105)

#### Starlink expansion

On July 16, 2021, SpaceX entered an agreement to acquire [Swarm Technologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swarm_Technologies "Swarm Technologies"), a private company building a [low Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit "Low Earth orbit") satellite constellation for communications with [Internet of things](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_of_things "Internet of things") (IoT) devices, for $524 million.[\[106\]](#cite_note-106)[\[107\]](#cite_note-107)

In December 2022, the U.S. [Federal Communications Commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission "Federal Communications Commission") (FCC) approved the launch of up to 7,500 of SpaceX's next-generation satellites in its Starlink internet network.[\[108\]](#cite_note-108)

In September 2025, SpaceX said it would purchase the rights to use some of [EchoStar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EchoStar "EchoStar")'s spectrum for $17 billion in a cash and stock deal. The company said it would use the spectrum as a foundation for Starlink's direct-to-cell business around the globe.[\[109\]](#cite_note-109)

#### Starlink in the Russo-Ukrainian war

In February 2022, SpaceX activated their [Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink "Starlink") [satellite internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_Internet_access "Satellite Internet access") service in Ukraine, to replace [internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_in_Ukraine "Internet in Ukraine") and [communication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications_in_Ukraine "Telecommunications in Ukraine") networks degraded or destroyed during the war.[\[110\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_elon-musk-b2024184-110)[\[111\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_BBC_News-2022-111)[\[112\]](#cite_note-112) Starlink provides critical service to Ukrainian civilians, government and military.[\[111\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_BBC_News-2022-111)[\[113\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_Sabbagh-2023a-113) It has aided humanitarian relief as well as defense and counterattacks on Russian positions.[\[114\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_The_Economist-2023-114)

Initially, SpaceX provided and funded Starlink services to Ukraine without charge.[\[115\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_Reuters-2022-115)[\[116\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_Luxmoore-2022-116)[\[117\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_Brodkin-2022-117) As of June 2023 Starlink expenses for Ukraine were covered by the [US Department of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense "United States Department of Defense").[\[118\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_Stone-2023-118) As of December 2023, Poland was the largest contributor of terminals.[\[119\]](#cite_note-119) SpaceX did not extend Starlink availability to [Russian-occupied areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian-occupied_territories_of_Ukraine "Russian-occupied territories of Ukraine") such as [Crimea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_occupation_of_Crimea "Russian occupation of Crimea").[\[120\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_Roulette-2023-120)[\[114\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_The_Economist-2023-114)

In 2022, Elon Musk denied a Ukrainian request to extend Starlink's coverage up to Russian-occupied Crimea during a counterattack on a Crimean port; providing service would have violated US sanctions.[\[121\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_:1-121) Media reports erroneously characterized it as Musk "turning off" Starlink coverage in Crimea.[\[122\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_Darcy-2023-122)[\[123\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_Capoot-2023-123) In late 2022, SpaceX announced [Starshield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starshield "SpaceX Starshield"), a program designed for government customers.[\[124\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_:4-124)[\[125\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_Porter-2023-125)

The use of Starlink to attack Russian targets was criticized by Russian officials. Russia's military repeatedly tried to disrupt Starlink services.[\[126\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_LIGA-2022a-126)[\[118\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_Stone-2023-118) Reportedly, the Russian military at one point obtained Starlink terminals through other countries.[\[127\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_Reuters-2024c-127) This unauthorized Starlink use was eventually blocked.[\[128\]](#cite_note-Starlink_in_the_Russo-Ukrainian_war_:0-128) In February 2026, Starlink eliminated service to all terminals in Ukraine not on a [whitelist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitelist "Whitelist"), in order to prevent Russia's fraudulent Starlink use. This significantly disrupted Russian military communications, which had come to rely on Starlink.[\[129\]](#cite_note-129)

#### Starship

[![refer to caption](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/3a/Starship_full_stack.jpg/250px-Starship_full_stack.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Starship_full_stack.jpg)

Starship in launch position

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/2/23/SpaceX_Crew-10_launches_aboard_a_SpaceX_Falcon_9_rocket_carrying_the_Dragon_spacecraft_at_Kennedy_Space_Center%2C_FL%2C_March_2025.jpg/250px-SpaceX_Crew-10_launches_aboard_a_SpaceX_Falcon_9_rocket_carrying_the_Dragon_spacecraft_at_Kennedy_Space_Center%2C_FL%2C_March_2025.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:SpaceX_Crew-10_launches_aboard_a_SpaceX_Falcon_9_rocket_carrying_the_Dragon_spacecraft_at_Kennedy_Space_Center,_FL,_March_2025.jpg)

Dragon's Flight: SpaceX Crew-10 launches aboard a SpaceX Falcon 9 rocket carrying the Dragon spacecraft piloted by astronaut and Air Force Maj. Nichole "Vapor" Ayer at Kennedy Space Center, FL, March 14, 2025.

On April 20, 2023, Starship's [first orbital flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship_orbital_test_flight "SpaceX Starship orbital test flight") ended in a mid-air explosion over the [Gulf of Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Mexico "Gulf of Mexico") before booster separation. After launch, multiple engines in the booster progressively failed, causing the vehicle to reach [max _q_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_q "Max q") later than planned. "Max _q_" is the point during ascent at which the vehicle experiences the maximum [aerodynamic pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamic_pressure "Dynamic pressure"), determined by the combination of air density and velocity as the rocket climbs through the atmosphere. Eventually, the vehicle lost control and spun erratically until the automated [flight termination system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flight_termination_system "Flight termination system") was activated, which intentionally destroyed the rocket. Elon Musk, SpaceX, and other individuals familiar with the space industry have referred to the test flight as a success.[\[130\]](#cite_note-130)[\[131\]](#cite_note-berger-ift1-fail-or-success-131)

Musk said at the time that it would take "six to eight weeks" to get the infrastructure prepared for another launch. In October 2023, a senior SpaceX executive stated the company had been ready to launch the next test flight since September. He accused government regulators of disrupting the project's progress, adding the delay could lead to China beating U.S. astronauts back to the Moon.[\[132\]](#cite_note-132)[\[133\]](#cite_note-133)

On November 18, 2023, SpaceX launched Starship on its [second flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_test_2 "Starship flight test 2"), with both vehicles flying for a few minutes before separately exploding.[\[134\]](#cite_note-134)[\[135\]](#cite_note-135)[\[136\]](#cite_note-136)[\[137\]](#cite_note-137)

In early March 2024 SpaceX announced that it was targeting March 14 as the tentative launch date for its next uncrewed Starship launch configuration flight test, pending the issuance of a "launch license" by the [FAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FAA "FAA"). This license was granted on March 13, 2024.[\[138\]](#cite_note-138) On March 14, 2024, at 13:25 UTC, [Starship launched for the third time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_test_3 "Starship flight test 3") and for the first time Starship reached its planned suborbital trajectory. The flight ended with the booster experiencing a malfunction shortly before landing and the ship being lost during re-entry over the Indian Ocean.[\[76\]](#cite_note-starship-flight-3-76)[\[77\]](#cite_note-starship-press-kit-77)

On June 4, 2024, SpaceX received the launch license for [Starship's fourth flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_test_4 "Starship flight test 4"). The licensure itself was notable in that it was the first time that the FAA included a clause that would allow SpaceX to launch subsequent test flights without a mishap investigation, provided that they met a similar launch profile and used the same specification of hardware. The provision could prove to speed the development timeline.[\[139\]](#cite_note-139)

On October 12, 2024, SpaceX received FAA approval for [Starship's fifth flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_test_5 "Starship flight test 5").[\[140\]](#cite_note-140) The flight was the first without engine failures, and the first successful tower catch.[\[141\]](#cite_note-nsf20241012-141)

SpaceX launched Starship on its [sixth flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_test_6 "Starship flight test 6") on November 19, 2024.[\[142\]](#cite_note-142) The booster aborted the catch attempt, while the ship conducted a relight in space.[\[143\]](#cite_note-:02-143)[\[144\]](#cite_note-144)

On January 16, 2025, SpaceX launched Starship on its [seventh flight test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_test_7 "Starship flight test 7"), with the first Block 2 Ship, Ship 33, standing at 403 feet (123 m). This test also carried a demonstration payload, a Starlink V3 simulator. The test launched at 22:37 UTC. The test resulted in the second catch of the Super Heavy booster, B14, but after 8 minutes, SpaceX lost contact with 'Ship', which is the upper stage of the Starship which resulted in the failure of the ship during the ascent. The spacecraft reportedly exploded around 8.5 minutes after launch over the Atlantic Ocean near the [Turks and Caicos Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turks_and_Caicos_Islands "Turks and Caicos Islands"). The FAA, on January 18, required a mishap investigation of the failure.[\[145\]](#cite_note-145)

On March 7, 2025, SpaceX launched another Starship rocket, this time from Texas. Contact was lost minutes into the test flight and the spacecraft came tumbling down and broke apart, with wreckage seen across Florida's skies.[\[146\]](#cite_note-146) As per preliminary investigation, Starship's 7th test flight was disrupted by an oxygen leak, flashes and sustained fires in its aft section, which caused the rocket's engines to shut down and turn on the spacecraft's self-destruct system.[\[147\]](#cite_note-147)

On June 18, 2025, a SpaceX Starship rocket exploded during a static fire test at the company's Starbase facility in Texas, following what the company described as a "major anomaly".[\[148\]](#cite_note-CBSStarshipExplosion-148) Cameron County Judge Eddie Treviño Jr. contacted 10 federal and state offices to ask about their roles in probes regarding the explosion, with each department declining to investigate, which created regulatory concerns. Brownsville City Manager Helen Ramirez stated that the Brownsville Fire Department was underequipped to handle fire events created by rocket explosions. Not long after the explosion, several SpaceX employees filed documents with the Texas Secretary of State to form the Starbase Volunteer Fire Department, intending to respond to calls within city limits, not including the test site. While cleaning the site, a crane collapse in relation to safety violations would cause the [Occupational Safety and Health Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_Safety_and_Health_Administration "Occupational Safety and Health Administration") to fine SpaceX approximately $116,000.[\[149\]](#cite_note-149)

On May 22, 2026, SpaceX successfully launched the [twelfth flight test of Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starship_flight_test_12 "Starship flight test 12"). This was the first flight of the V3 vehicle, incorporating [Raptor 3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Raptor#Raptor_3 "SpaceX Raptor") engines. It was also the first flight test to carry a [payload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payload "Payload") into orbit, lofting approximately 44,000 kg (97,000 lbs) of Starlink mass simulators. During launch, one of the 33 Raptor 3 engines on the Super Heavy first stage shut down early, and one of the 6 Starship upper-stage engines shut down prematurely, but despite the engine-out, the vehicle achieved its planned suborbital trajectory.[\[150\]](#cite_note-150) The booster suffered a subsequent anomaly and was destroyed before splashdown over the [Gulf of Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_of_Mexico "Gulf of Mexico"), while the ship safely completed its [orbital pass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_pass "Orbital pass") and achieved a targeted [soft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_landing "Soft landing") [splashdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splashdown "Splashdown") in the Indian Ocean near Australia.

#### xAI acquisition, Terafab, and Starfall

On February 2, 2026, SpaceX announced that it had acquired [xAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceXAI "SpaceXAI"), an artificial intelligence company also founded by Musk, in an all-stock transaction that structured xAI as a wholly owned subsidiary of SpaceX.[\[151\]](#cite_note-151) The acquisition combines SpaceX's rocket and satellite capabilities with xAI's artificial intelligence technology. SpaceX stated that the goal is to develop space-based AI data centers to overcome the power and cooling limitations of terrestrial facilities. The plan includes deploying large numbers of satellites to provide AI compute capacity and exploring long-term operations on the Moon and Mars.[\[152\]](#cite_note-152) The acquisition valued SpaceX at $1T and xAI at $250B, for a combined total of $1.25T. It was the highest valued business acquisition in history.[\[153\]](#cite_note-153)[\[154\]](#cite_note-acquiresxAI-154)

In March, SpaceX announced the project to build [Terafab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terafab "Terafab"), a joint project with [Tesla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla,_Inc. "Tesla, Inc.") and [xAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceXAI "SpaceXAI"), to build a large [semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiconductor_industry "Semiconductor industry") fabrication project centering on the construction of a vertically integrated "mega-fab" designed to produce more than one [terawatt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terawatt "Terawatt") (one trillion watts) of [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence "Artificial intelligence") compute capacity per year, encompassing advanced [logic chips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit "Integrated circuit"), [memory modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_memory "Computer memory"), and [packaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multi-chip_module "Multi-chip module") technologies. They will initially build a much smaller fast-iteration semiconductor fab, with all functions under one roof, at the existing [Giga Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gigafactory_Texas "Gigafactory Texas") property near [Austin, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin,_Texas "Austin, Texas").[\[155\]](#cite_note-155)[\[156\]](#cite_note-bi20260321-156) The following month, _[The New York Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_New_York_Times "The New York Times")_ reported that SpaceX had agreed to acquire [Cursor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursor_\(code_editor\) "Cursor (code editor)"), an AI-assisted software development startup, for $60 billion.[\[157\]](#cite_note-157)

In May 2026, [Anthropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropic "Anthropic") signed a contract of $1.25 billion per month with [xAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XAI_\(company\) "XAI (company)"), a subsidiary of SpaceX, to buy all the compute capacity at the Colossus 1 [data center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_center "Data center") in Memphis, Tennessee.[\[158\]](#cite_note-158)[\[159\]](#cite_note-:03-159) According to a June securities filing, Google and SpaceX agreed on a computational rent agreement. Google will pay $920 million a month to SpaceX for the use of [Nvidia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nvidia "Nvidia") chips from October 2026 until June 2029.[\[160\]](#cite_note-160)

On May 15, the company received FAA approval for its [Starfall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceXAI_Starfall "SpaceXAI Starfall") orbit-to-Earth delivery capsule.[\[161\]](#cite_note-161)[\[162\]](#cite_note-162)

#### Initial public offering

In December 2025, Elon Musk confirmed that [SpaceX would go public](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_public_offering_of_SpaceX "Initial public offering of SpaceX"), after previously rejecting the idea.[\[163\]](#cite_note-163) In January 2026, four banks were selected to lead the IPO.[\[164\]](#cite_note-164)[\[165\]](#cite_note-165)[\[166\]](#cite_note-166)[\[167\]](#cite_note-167) The shareholding structure effectively prevents anyone but Musk from firing him from his role as chief executive and board chair.[\[168\]](#cite_note-168)

On May 20, 2026, [SpaceX filed for an initial public offering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_public_offering_of_SpaceX "Initial public offering of SpaceX") (IPO) on [Nasdaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasdaq "Nasdaq") under the ticker symbol SPCX.[\[169\]](#cite_note-169)[\[170\]](#cite_note-:2-170) On June 11, 2026, SpaceX priced its IPO at $135 per share, giving the company a valuation of $1.77 trillion, making it the highest-valued IPO ever.[\[170\]](#cite_note-:2-170) The offering raised $75 billion, making it the largest IPO by proceeds on record.[\[171\]](#cite_note-:3-171) It began trading on the Nasdaq on June 12 under the ticker symbol SPCX.[\[171\]](#cite_note-:3-171)

#### Starmind

SpaceXAI is in the development phase for a solar-powered [space-based data center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space-based_data_center "Space-based data center") named Starmind. Starmind is self-described by the company as an "orbital satellite with localized compute that takes advantage of readily available power and cooling in space and beams back data via high-bandwidth lasers to the Starlink constellation." The first-generation Starmind AI1 satellite is to have a wingspan of 75 m (246 ft 1 in) and a deployed height of 30 m (98 ft 5 in), with up to 250 kW peak and 175 kW average [compute payload](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Payload_\(computing\) "Payload (computing)").[\[172\]](#cite_note-172) In August 2026, Nvidia announced the internal compute of the satellite would be based on an optimized [Nvidia Vera Rubin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubin_\(microarchitecture\) "Rubin (microarchitecture)") (NVL72) system.[\[173\]](#cite_note-173)

## Hardware

### Launch vehicles

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/83/CRS-18_Mission_%2848380511427%29.jpg/250px-CRS-18_Mission_%2848380511427%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:CRS-18_Mission_\(48380511427\).jpg)

The landing of a [Falcon 9 Block 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Block_5 "Falcon 9 Block 5") first stage at Cape Canaveral in July 2019. [VTVL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VTVL "VTVL") technologies are used in many of SpaceX's launch vehicles.

SpaceX has developed three launch vehicles. The small-lift [Falcon 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_1 "Falcon 1") was the first launch vehicle developed and was retired in 2009. The medium-lift [Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9 "Falcon 9") and the heavy-lift [Falcon Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy "Falcon Heavy") are both operational.

[Falcon 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_1 "Falcon 1") was a small rocket capable of placing several hundred kilograms into [low Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit "Low Earth orbit"). It launched five times between 2006 and 2009, of which two were successful.[\[174\]](#cite_note-Falcon9_overview-174) The Falcon 1 was the first privately funded, liquid-fueled rocket to reach orbit.[\[175\]](#cite_note-nasa2008-175)

[Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9 "Falcon 9") is a [medium-lift launch vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medium-lift_launch_vehicle "Medium-lift launch vehicle") capable of delivering up to 22,800 kilograms (50,265 lb) to orbit, competing with the [Delta IV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_IV "Delta IV") and the [Atlas V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlas_V "Atlas V") rockets, as well as other launch providers around the world. It has nine [Merlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Merlin "SpaceX Merlin") engines in its first stage. The [Falcon 9 v1.0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_v1.0 "Falcon 9 v1.0") rocket successfully reached orbit on its first attempt on June 4, 2010. Its third flight, [COTS Demo Flight 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COTS_Demo_Flight_2 "COTS Demo Flight 2"), launched on May 22, 2012, and launched the first [commercial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_spaceflight "Private spaceflight") spacecraft to reach and dock with the International Space Station (ISS).[\[46\]](#cite_note-cbcnews-46) The vehicle was upgraded to [Falcon 9 v1.1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_v1.1 "Falcon 9 v1.1") in 2013, [Falcon 9 Full Thrust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Full_Thrust "Falcon 9 Full Thrust") in 2015, and finally to [Falcon 9 Block 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Block_5 "Falcon 9 Block 5") in 2018. The first stage of Falcon 9 is designed to retro propulsively land, be recovered, and flown again.[\[176\]](#cite_note-176)

[Falcon Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy "Falcon Heavy") is a [heavy-lift launch vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy-lift_launch_vehicle "Heavy-lift launch vehicle") capable of delivering up to 63,800 kg (140,700 lb) to [Low Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit "Low Earth orbit") (LEO) or 26,700 kg (58,900 lb) to [geosynchronous transfer orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geosynchronous_transfer_orbit "Geosynchronous transfer orbit") (GTO). It uses three slightly modified Falcon 9 first-stage cores with a total of 27 [Merlin 1D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Merlin "SpaceX Merlin") engines.[\[177\]](#cite_note-sxfh2011-177)[\[178\]](#cite_note-nsw20130103-178) The Falcon Heavy successfully flew its inaugural mission on February 6, 2018, launching Musk's personal [Tesla Roadster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tesla_Roadster_\(2008\) "Tesla Roadster (2008)") into [heliocentric orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric_orbit "Heliocentric orbit").[\[179\]](#cite_note-179)

Both the Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy are certified to conduct launches for the [National Security Space Launch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Space_Launch "National Security Space Launch") (NSSL).[\[180\]](#cite_note-NSSL1-180)[\[181\]](#cite_note-181) As of September 26, 2026, the Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy have been launched 703 times, resulting in 700 full mission successes, one partial success, and [one in-flight failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_CRS-7 "SpaceX CRS-7"). In addition, a Falcon 9 experienced [a pre-flight failure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AMOS-6#Destruction "AMOS-6") before a static fire test in 2016.[\[182\]](#cite_note-182)[\[183\]](#cite_note-183)

SpaceX is developing a fully reusable super-heavy lift launch system known as [Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship "SpaceX Starship"). It comprises a reusable first stage, called [Super Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Super_Heavy "SpaceX Super Heavy"), and the reusable [Starship second stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship_\(spacecraft\) "SpaceX Starship (spacecraft)") space vehicle. As of 2017, the system was intended to supersede the company's existing launch vehicle hardware by the early 2020s.[\[184\]](#cite_note-nsf20170929-184)[\[185\]](#cite_note-185)

### Rocket engines

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/44/SpaceX_Testing_Merlin_1D_Engine_In_Texas.jpg/250px-SpaceX_Testing_Merlin_1D_Engine_In_Texas.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:SpaceX_Testing_Merlin_1D_Engine_In_Texas.jpg)

[Merlin 1D engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merlin_\(rocket_engine_family\)#Merlin_1D "Merlin (rocket engine family)") undergoes a test at SpaceX's Rocket Development and Test Facility in [McGregor, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McGregor,_Texas "McGregor, Texas")

Since the founding of SpaceX in 2002, the company has developed several [rocket engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rocket_engine "Rocket engine") – [Merlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Merlin "SpaceX Merlin"), [Kestrel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Kestrel "SpaceX Kestrel"), and [Raptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Raptor "SpaceX Raptor") – for use in [launch vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_vehicle "Launch vehicle"),[\[186\]](#cite_note-guzzling-186)[\[187\]](#cite_note-falcon1k-187) [Draco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Draco "SpaceX Draco") for the [reaction control system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_control_system "Reaction control system") of the Dragon series of spacecraft,[\[188\]](#cite_note-Guide-188) and [SuperDraco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SuperDraco "SuperDraco") for abort capability in [Crew Dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon "Crew Dragon").[\[189\]](#cite_note-189)

Merlin is a family of rocket engines that uses [liquid oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_oxygen "Liquid oxygen") (LOX) and [RP-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RP-1 "RP-1") propellants. Merlin was first used to power the Falcon 1's first stage and is now used on both stages of the Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy vehicles.[\[190\]](#cite_note-190) Kestrel uses the same propellants and was used as the Falcon 1 rocket's second-stage main engine.[\[187\]](#cite_note-falcon1k-187)[\[191\]](#cite_note-good_boy_astro-191)

Draco and [SuperDraco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SuperDraco "SuperDraco") are [hypergolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergolic "Hypergolic") liquid-propellant rocket engines. Draco engines are used on the [reaction control system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_control_system "Reaction control system") of the [Dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Dragon "SpaceX Dragon") and [Dragon 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_2 "Dragon 2") spacecraft.[\[188\]](#cite_note-Guide-188) The SuperDraco engine is more powerful, and eight SuperDraco engines provide [launch escape capability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_escape_system "Launch escape system") for crewed Dragon 2 spacecraft during an abort scenario.[\[192\]](#cite_note-192)

Raptor is a family of liquid oxygen and liquid [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane "Methane")\-fueled [full-flow staged combustion cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full-flow_staged_combustion_cycle "Full-flow staged combustion cycle") engines to power the first and second stages of the in-development [Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship "SpaceX Starship") launch system.[\[186\]](#cite_note-guzzling-186) Development versions were test-fired in late 2016,[\[193\]](#cite_note-193) and the engine flew for the first time in 2019, powering the _Starhopper_ vehicle to an altitude of 20 m (66 ft).[\[194\]](#cite_note-194)

### Dragon spacecraft

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/11/Iss065e162284.jpg/250px-Iss065e162284.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Iss065e162284.jpg)

[SpaceX Dragon 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Dragon_2 "SpaceX Dragon 2") spacecraft, designed to deliver crew and cargo to and from the International Space Station (cargo version shown)

SpaceX has developed the Dragon spacecraft to transport cargo and crew to the International Space Station (ISS).

The first-generation [Dragon 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_1 "Dragon 1") spacecraft was used only for cargo operations. It was developed with financial support from NASA under the [Commercial Orbital Transportation Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Orbital_Transportation_Services "Commercial Orbital Transportation Services") (COTS) program. After a successful COTS demonstration flight in 2010, SpaceX was chosen to receive a [Commercial Resupply Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Resupply_Services "Commercial Resupply Services") (CRS) contract.[\[39\]](#cite_note-splash-39)

The currently operational second-generation [Dragon 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_2 "Dragon 2") spacecraft conducted [its first flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon_Demo-1 "Crew Dragon Demo-1"), without crew, to the ISS in early 2019, followed by [a crewed flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon_Demo-2 "Crew Dragon Demo-2") of Dragon 2 in 2020.[\[85\]](#cite_note-Lifts-85) It was developed with financial support from NASA under the [Commercial Crew Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Crew_Program "Commercial Crew Program") program. The [cargo variant of Dragon 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_2#Cargo_Dragon "Dragon 2") flew for the first time in December 2020, for a resupply to the ISS as part of the CRS contract with NASA.[\[195\]](#cite_note-195)

In March 2020 SpaceX revealed the Dragon XL, designed as a resupply spacecraft for NASA's planned [Lunar Gateway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Gateway "Lunar Gateway") space station under a [Gateway Logistics Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gateway_Logistics_Services "Gateway Logistics Services") (GLS) contract.[\[196\]](#cite_note-196) Dragon XL is planned to launch on the [Falcon Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy "Falcon Heavy"), and is able to transport over 5,000 kg (11,000 lb) to the Gateway. Dragon XL will be docked at the Gateway for six to twelve months at a time.[\[197\]](#cite_note-197)

[SpaceX designed a spacesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_suit#SpaceX_suit_\("Starman_suit"\) "Space suit") to be worn inside the Dragon spacecraft to protect from possible depressurization.[\[198\]](#cite_note-198) In May 2024, SpaceX unveiled a second spacesuit designed for [extravehicular activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extravehicular_activity "Extravehicular activity"), planned to be used for a [spacewalk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacewalk "Spacewalk") during the [Polaris Dawn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaris_Dawn "Polaris Dawn") mission.[\[199\]](#cite_note-199)

### Autonomous spaceport drone ships

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/97/SpaceX_ASDS_in_position_prior_to_Falcon_9_Flight_17_carrying_CRS-6_%2817127808431%29.jpg/250px-SpaceX_ASDS_in_position_prior_to_Falcon_9_Flight_17_carrying_CRS-6_%2817127808431%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:SpaceX_ASDS_in_position_prior_to_Falcon_9_Flight_17_carrying_CRS-6_\(17127808431\).jpg)

[Autonomous spaceport drone ship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship "Autonomous spaceport drone ship") in position prior to [CRS-6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CRS-6 "CRS-6") mission

SpaceX routinely returns the [first stage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_stage_\(rocketry\) "First stage (rocketry)") of Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy rockets after orbital launches. The rocket lands at a predetermined landing site using only its propulsion systems.[\[200\]](#cite_note-200) When propellant margins do not permit a return to a launch site (RTLS), rockets return to a floating landing platform in the ocean, called [autonomous spaceport drone ships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_spaceport_drone_ship "Autonomous spaceport drone ship") (ASDS).[\[201\]](#cite_note-201)

SpaceX also had plans to introduce [floating launch platforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_launch_platform "Floating launch platform"), which would be modified oil rigs to provide a sea launch option for their [Starship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starship "SpaceX Starship") launch vehicle. As of February 2023, SpaceX had sold the oil rigs, but had not ruled out sea-based platforms for future use.[\[202\]](#cite_note-202)

### Starlink

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/91/Starlink_Mission_%2847926144123%29.jpg/250px-Starlink_Mission_%2847926144123%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Starlink_Mission_\(47926144123\).jpg)

Sixty Starlink satellites stacked together before deployment

Starlink is an internet satellite constellation under development by Starlink Services, LLC, a wholly owned subsidiary of SpaceX,[\[203\]](#cite_note-StarlinkServices-203)[\[204\]](#cite_note-204) that consists of thousands of cross-linked [communications satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_satellite "Communications satellite") in ~550 km orbits. Its goal is to address the significant unmet demand worldwide for low-cost broadband capabilities.[\[205\]](#cite_note-sn20150119-205) Development began in 2015, and initial prototype test-flight satellites were launched on the SpaceX [Paz satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paz_\(satellite\) "Paz (satellite)") mission in 2017. In May 2019, SpaceX launched the first batch of 60 satellites aboard a Falcon 9.[\[206\]](#cite_note-206) Initial test operation of the constellation began in late 2020[\[207\]](#cite_note-207) and first orders were taken in early 2021.[\[208\]](#cite_note-208) Customers were told to expect internet service speeds of 50 Mbit/s to 150 Mbit/s and latency from 20 ms to 40 ms.[\[209\]](#cite_note-209) In December 2022, Starlink reached over 1 million subscribers worldwide.[\[210\]](#cite_note-210)

The planned large number of [Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink "Starlink") satellites has been criticized by astronomers due to concerns over [light pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_pollution "Light pollution"),[\[211\]](#cite_note-211)[\[212\]](#cite_note-212)[\[213\]](#cite_note-213) with the brightness of Starlink satellites in both optical and radio wavelengths interfering with scientific observations.[\[214\]](#cite_note-214) In response, SpaceX implemented several upgrades to Starlink satellites aimed at reducing their brightness.[\[215\]](#cite_note-215) The large number of satellites employed by Starlink also creates long-term dangers of [satellite collisions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_collision "Satellite collision") with [space debris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_debris "Space debris").[\[216\]](#cite_note-216)[\[217\]](#cite_note-217) However, the satellites are equipped with [krypton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krypton "Krypton")\-fueled [Hall thrusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hall-effect_thruster "Hall-effect thruster") which allow them to [de-orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De-orbit "De-orbit") at the end of their life. They are also designed to autonomously avoid collisions based on uplinked tracking data.[\[218\]](#cite_note-218)

In December 2022, SpaceX announced [Starshield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starshield_\(satellite_constellation\) "Starshield (satellite constellation)"), a program to incorporate military or government entity payloads on board a Starlink-derived [satellite bus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_bus "Satellite bus"). The [Space Development Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Development_Agency "Space Development Agency") is a key customer procuring satellites for a space-based [missile defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missile_defense "Missile defense") system.[\[219\]](#cite_note-219)

In June 2024, SpaceX introduced a compact version of its Starlink antennas, the "Starlink Mini", designed for mobile satellite internet use. Offered for $599 in an early access release, it was more expensive than the base model. The Mini antenna, half the size and one-third the weight of the Standard version, featured a built-in [WiFi router](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WiFi_router "WiFi router"), lower power consumption, and over 100 Mbit/s download speeds.[\[220\]](#cite_note-220)

## Facilities

SpaceX is headquartered at [SpaceX Starbase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starbase "SpaceX Starbase") near [Brownsville, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownsville,_Texas "Brownsville, Texas"), where it manufactures and launches its Starship launch vehicle. Much of the company's engineering, manufacturing, and mission operations remain based at its facility in [Hawthorne, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawthorne,_California "Hawthorne, California"), its headquarters until 2024, where it manufactures Falcon launch vehicles and Dragon spacecraft and operates mission control. The company also operates a rocket development and test facility in [McGregor, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McGregor,_Texas "McGregor, Texas"),[\[221\]](#cite_note-221) and an office in the Washington, D.C., area that supports government customers.[\[222\]](#cite_note-222) SpaceX operates launch facilities in California, Florida, and Texas.

### Hawthorne, California

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/ee/Iridium-4_Mission_%2825557986177%29.jpg/250px-Iridium-4_Mission_%2825557986177%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Iridium-4_Mission_\(25557986177\).jpg)

SpaceX office in Hawthorne, California, during a Falcon 9 launch from [Vandenberg Space Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandenberg_Space_Force_Base "Vandenberg Space Force Base")

SpaceX operates a facility in [Hawthorne, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hawthorne,_California "Hawthorne, California"). The three-story building, originally constructed by [Northrop Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northrop_Corporation "Northrop Corporation") to manufacture [Boeing 747](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boeing_747 "Boeing 747") [fuselage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fuselage "Fuselage") sections,[\[223\]](#cite_note-pm-223) houses office space, mission control, and Falcon 9 manufacturing operations.[\[224\]](#cite_note-arstechnica_2019-224)

SpaceX manufactures its rocket engines, rocket stages, spacecraft, [avionics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avionics "Avionics"), and flight software largely in-house.[\[20\]](#cite_note-Changing-20) Much of this work is performed at the Hawthorne facility, reflecting the company's emphasis on [vertical integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_integration "Vertical integration").[\[20\]](#cite_note-Changing-20)

The Hawthorne facility served as SpaceX's headquarters until August 2024, when the company relocated its headquarters to Starbase, Texas.[\[225\]](#cite_note-Clark_2024-225)

### Starbase, Texas

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/4/4c/The_Boca_Chica_Rocket_Factory.jpg/250px-The_Boca_Chica_Rocket_Factory.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:The_Boca_Chica_Rocket_Factory.jpg)

The Starship assembly building at [SpaceX Starbase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starbase "SpaceX Starbase")

SpaceX manufactures and launches Starship vehicles from [SpaceX Starbase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starbase "SpaceX Starbase") in [Boca Chica, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boca_Chica,_Texas "Boca Chica, Texas"), near [Brownsville, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownsville,_Texas "Brownsville, Texas"). The company announced plans for the site in 2014,[\[226\]](#cite_note-226)[\[227\]](#cite_note-227) and began construction later that year.[\[228\]](#cite_note-sn20140922-228) The first suborbital test flights occurred in 2019,[\[224\]](#cite_note-arstechnica_2019-224) followed by orbital launches beginning in 2023.

SpaceX's operations at Starbase have been the subject of environmental reviews and regulatory actions. In August 2024, the [Texas Commission on Environmental Quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Texas_Commission_on_Environmental_Quality "Texas Commission on Environmental Quality") cited the company for environmental violations related to discharges near the launch site.[\[229\]](#cite_note-229) The [Environmental Protection Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_Protection_Agency "Environmental Protection Agency") later fined the company approximately $150,000 for alleged violations of the [Clean Water Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clean_Water_Act "Clean Water Act").[\[230\]](#cite_note-230)

### McGregor, Texas

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7b/2008_SpaceX%27s_Rocket_Development_facility%2C_McGregor%2C_TX.jpg/250px-2008_SpaceX%27s_Rocket_Development_facility%2C_McGregor%2C_TX.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:2008_SpaceX's_Rocket_Development_facility,_McGregor,_TX.jpg)

Aerial view of the SpaceX rocket test facility in McGregor, Texas

The [SpaceX Rocket Development and Test Facility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Rocket_Development_and_Test_Facility "SpaceX Rocket Development and Test Facility") in [McGregor, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McGregor,_Texas "McGregor, Texas"), is used for rocket engine development and testing. Every SpaceX rocket engine and thruster undergoes acceptance testing at the facility before flight.[\[231\]](#cite_note-wired201210102-231)[\[232\]](#cite_note-nsfdc20130620-232) The site is also used for research, development, and qualification testing of engines and other propulsion components.[\[233\]](#cite_note-wt20111222-233)

Following splashdown and recovery, Dragon spacecraft are transported to McGregor for removal of residual hypergolic propellants before refurbishment elsewhere.[\[231\]](#cite_note-wired201210102-231)

According to SpaceX, more than 7,000 tests had been conducted at the facility by 2024 across more than a dozen test stands.[\[234\]](#cite_note-shotwell201503317-234) Company president and chief operating officer [Gwynne Shotwell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gwynne_Shotwell "Gwynne Shotwell") maintains an office at the site.[\[235\]](#cite_note-:0-235)

The property was originally part of the [Bluebonnet Ordnance Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluebonnet_Ordnance_Plant "Bluebonnet Ordnance Plant") during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II "World War II").[\[233\]](#cite_note-wt20111222-233) It was later used by [Beal Aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beal_Aerospace "Beal Aerospace") before being leased by SpaceX in 2003.[\[236\]](#cite_note-236) SpaceX expanded the facility from approximately 256 acres (104 ha) in 2003 to 4,000 acres (1,600 ha) by 2015.[\[233\]](#cite_note-wt20111222-233)[\[234\]](#cite_note-shotwell201503317-234)

In 2021, SpaceX announced plans to construct an additional Raptor engine production facility at McGregor to increase manufacturing capacity.[\[237\]](#cite_note-dallasnews202107-237)[\[238\]](#cite_note-spacex20210710-238)

### Launch facilities

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/52/Falcon_Heavy_Side_Boosters_landing_on_LZ1_and_LZ2_-_2018_%2825254688767%29.jpg/250px-Falcon_Heavy_Side_Boosters_landing_on_LZ1_and_LZ2_-_2018_%2825254688767%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Falcon_Heavy_Side_Boosters_landing_on_LZ1_and_LZ2_-_2018_\(25254688767\).jpg)

Falcon Heavy Side Boosters landing on [LZ1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_Zones_1_and_2 "Landing Zones 1 and 2") and LZ2 at Cape Canaveral

SpaceX operates four [orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_spaceflight "Orbital spaceflight") launch sites, at [Cape Canaveral Space Force Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Canaveral_Space_Force_Station "Cape Canaveral Space Force Station") and [Kennedy Space Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Space_Center "Kennedy Space Center") in Florida and [Vandenberg Space Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandenberg_Space_Force_Base "Vandenberg Space Force Base") in California for Falcon rockets, and [Starbase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Starbase "SpaceX Starbase") near [Brownsville, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brownsville,_Texas "Brownsville, Texas") for Starship. SpaceX has indicated that they see a niche for each of the four orbital facilities and that they have sufficient launch business to fill each pad.[\[239\]](#cite_note-sn20130802-239) The Vandenberg launch site enables highly inclined orbits (66–145°), while Cape Canaveral and Kennedy enable orbits of medium inclination (28.5–55°).[\[240\]](#cite_note-240) Larger inclinations, including [SSO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun-synchronous_orbit "Sun-synchronous orbit"), are possible from Florida by overflying Cuba.[\[241\]](#cite_note-241)

Before it was retired, all [Falcon 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_1 "Falcon 1") launches took place at the [Ronald Reagan Ballistic Missile Defense Test Site](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Reagan_Ballistic_Missile_Defense_Test_Site "Ronald Reagan Ballistic Missile Defense Test Site") on [Omelek Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omelek_Island "Omelek Island") of the [Marshall Islands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marshall_Islands "Marshall Islands").[\[242\]](#cite_note-242)

In April 2007, [the Pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Pentagon "The Pentagon") approved the use of [Cape Canaveral Space Launch Complex 40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Canaveral_Space_Launch_Complex_40 "Cape Canaveral Space Launch Complex 40") (SLC-40) by SpaceX.[\[243\]](#cite_note-243) The site has been used since 2010 for Falcon 9 launches, mainly to low Earth and geostationary orbits.

The former [Launch Complex 13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_Complex_13 "Launch Complex 13") at Cape Canaveral, renamed [Landing Zones 1 and 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_Zones_1_and_2 "Landing Zones 1 and 2"), was used from 2015 until its retirement in 2026 for [Falcon 9 first-stage booster landings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_first-stage_landing_tests "Falcon 9 first-stage landing tests").[\[244\]](#cite_note-244) The landing zones were retired in 2026 after the Space Force leased Launch Complex 13 to a new customer. SpaceX has constructed new return-to-launch-site landing facilities at Launch Complex 39A and Space Launch Complex 40.[\[245\]](#cite_note-245)[\[246\]](#cite_note-246)[\[247\]](#cite_note-247)

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/58/Launch_of_Falcon_9_carrying_CASSIOPE_%28130929-F-ET475-012%29.jpg/250px-Launch_of_Falcon_9_carrying_CASSIOPE_%28130929-F-ET475-012%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Launch_of_Falcon_9_carrying_CASSIOPE_\(130929-F-ET475-012\).jpg)

SpaceX west coast launch facility at [Vandenberg Space Force Base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandenberg_Space_Force_Base "Vandenberg Space Force Base"), during the launch of [CASSIOPE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CASSIOPE "CASSIOPE")

[Vandenberg Space Launch Complex 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vandenberg_Space_Launch_Complex_4 "Vandenberg Space Launch Complex 4") (SLC-4E) was leased from the military in 2011 and is used for payloads to polar orbits. The Vandenberg site can launch both Falcon 9 and Falcon Heavy vehicles,[\[248\]](#cite_note-nsf20110405-248) but cannot launch to low inclination orbits. The neighboring SLC-4W was converted to [Landing Zone 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landing_Zone_4 "Landing Zone 4") in 2015 for booster landings.[\[249\]](#cite_note-249)

On April 14, 2014, SpaceX signed a 20-year lease for [Kennedy Space Center Launch Complex 39A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Space_Center_Launch_Complex_39A "Kennedy Space Center Launch Complex 39A").[\[250\]](#cite_note-250) The pad was subsequently modified to support [Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9 "Falcon 9") and [Falcon Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy "Falcon Heavy") launches. As of 2024 it is the only pad that supports Falcon Heavy launches. SpaceX launched its [first crewed mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon_Demo-2 "Crew Dragon Demo-2") to the ISS from Launch Pad 39A on May 30, 2020.[\[251\]](#cite_note-251) Pad 39A has been prepared since 2019 to eventually accommodate Starship launches. With delays in launch FAA permits for Boca Chica, Texas, the 39A Starship preparation was accelerated in 2022.[\[252\]](#cite_note-252)

In August 2026, _The New York Times_ reported that SpaceX planned a second private spaceport in Louisiana, described as a multi-pad Starship complex after Starbase in Texas.[\[253\]](#cite_note-NYT-LA-253)[\[254\]](#cite_note-AW-LA-254)

On August 30, 2026, a Falcon Heavy launched NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope from Kennedy Space Center Launch Complex 39A.[\[255\]](#cite_note-SFN-Roman-255)

## Contracts

SpaceX won demonstration and actual supply contracts from [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA") for the International Space Station (ISS) with technology the company developed. SpaceX is also certified for [U.S. military](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._military "U.S. military") launches of [Evolved Expendable Launch Vehicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolved_Expendable_Launch_Vehicle "Evolved Expendable Launch Vehicle")\-class (EELV) payloads.

### Cargo to International Space Station (ISS)

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/cf/COTS2_Dragon_is_berthed.jpg/250px-COTS2_Dragon_is_berthed.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:COTS2_Dragon_is_berthed.jpg)

The COTS 2 Dragon is berthed to the [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station "International Space Station") (ISS) by [Canadarm2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobile_Servicing_System "Mobile Servicing System")

In 2006, SpaceX won a NASA [Commercial Orbital Transportation Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Orbital_Transportation_Services "Commercial Orbital Transportation Services") (COTS) Phase 1 contract to demonstrate cargo delivery to the ISS, with a possible contract option for crew transport.[\[256\]](#cite_note-256) Through this contract, designed by NASA to provide "seed money" through Space Act Agreements for developing new capabilities, NASA paid SpaceX $396 million to develop the cargo configuration of the Dragon spacecraft, while SpaceX developed the Falcon 9 launch vehicle with their resources.[\[257\]](#cite_note-257) These [Space Act Agreements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Act_Agreement "Space Act Agreement") have been shown to have saved NASA millions of dollars in development costs, making rocket development 4–10 times less expensive than if produced by NASA alone.[\[258\]](#cite_note-Zapata_2017-258)

In December 2010, with the launch of the [SpaceX COTS Demo Flight 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_COTS_Demo_Flight_1 "SpaceX COTS Demo Flight 1") mission, SpaceX became the first private company to successfully launch, orbit, and recover a spacecraft.[\[259\]](#cite_note-259) Dragon successfully berthed with the ISS during [SpaceX COTS Demo Flight 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_COTS_Demo_Flight_2 "SpaceX COTS Demo Flight 2") in May 2012, a first for a [private spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_spacecraft "Private spacecraft").[\[260\]](#cite_note-260)

[Commercial Resupply Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Resupply_Services "Commercial Resupply Services") (CRS) is a series of contracts awarded by NASA from 2008 to 2016 for the delivery of cargo and supplies to the ISS on commercially operated spacecraft. The first CRS contracts were signed in 2008 and awarded $1.6 billion to SpaceX for 12 cargo transport missions, covering deliveries to 2016.[\[261\]](#cite_note-push-261) [SpaceX CRS-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_CRS-1 "SpaceX CRS-1"), the first of the 12 planned resupply missions, launched in October 2012, achieved orbit, berthed, and remained on station for 20 days, before [re-entering the atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_re-entry "Atmospheric re-entry") and splashing down in the Pacific Ocean.[\[262\]](#cite_note-nasa20121028-262)

CRS missions have flown approximately twice a year to the ISS since then. In 2015, NASA extended the Phase 1 contracts by ordering an additional three resupply flights from SpaceX, and then extended the contract further for a total of twenty cargo missions to the ISS.[\[263\]](#cite_note-263)[\[261\]](#cite_note-push-261)[\[264\]](#cite_note-264) The final Dragon 1 mission, [SpaceX CRS-20](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_CRS-20 "SpaceX CRS-20"), departed the ISS in April 2020, and [Dragon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Dragon "SpaceX Dragon") was subsequently retired from service. A [second phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commercial_Resupply_Services_2 "Commercial Resupply Services 2") of contracts was awarded in January 2016 with SpaceX as one of the awardees. SpaceX will fly up to nine additional CRS flights with the upgraded [Dragon 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_2 "Dragon 2") spacecraft.[\[265\]](#cite_note-NASA-SMSR-265)[\[266\]](#cite_note-266) In March 2020, NASA contracted SpaceX to develop the [Dragon XL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_XL "Dragon XL") spacecraft to send supplies to the [Lunar Gateway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_Gateway "Lunar Gateway") space station. Dragon XL will be launched on a Falcon Heavy.[\[267\]](#cite_note-267)

### Crewing

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/f/f1/NASA_astronauts_work_aboard_the_SpaceX_Crew_Dragon_%28iss064e004754%29.jpg/250px-NASA_astronauts_work_aboard_the_SpaceX_Crew_Dragon_%28iss064e004754%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:NASA_astronauts_work_aboard_the_SpaceX_Crew_Dragon_\(iss064e004754\).jpg)

NASA astronauts inside the Dragon spacecraft during the [Crew-1 mission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SpaceX_Crew-1 "SpaceX Crew-1") rendezvous with the International Space Station

SpaceX is responsible for the transportation of NASA astronauts to and from the ISS. The NASA contracts started as part of the Commercial Crew Development (CCDev) program, aimed at developing commercially operated spacecraft capable of delivering astronauts to the ISS. The first contract was awarded to SpaceX in 2011,[\[268\]](#cite_note-268)[\[269\]](#cite_note-269) followed by another in 2012 to continue development and testing of its [Dragon 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_2 "Dragon 2") spacecraft.[\[270\]](#cite_note-August_2012-270)

In September 2014, NASA chose SpaceX and Boeing as the two companies that would be funded to develop systems to transport U.S. crews to and from the ISS.[\[271\]](#cite_note-September_2014-271) SpaceX won $2.6 billion to complete and certify Dragon 2 by 2017. The contracts called for at least one crewed flight test with at least one NASA astronaut aboard. Once Crew Dragon received NASA human-spaceflight certification, the contract required SpaceX to conduct at least two, and as many as six, crewed missions to the space station.[\[271\]](#cite_note-September_2014-271)

SpaceX completed the first key flight test of its Crew Dragon spacecraft, a [Pad Abort Test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon_Pad_Abort_Test "Crew Dragon Pad Abort Test"), in May 2015,[\[272\]](#cite_note-272) and successfully conducted a full uncrewed test flight in March 2019. The capsule docked to the ISS and then splashed down in the Atlantic Ocean.[\[273\]](#cite_note-273) In January 2020, SpaceX conducted an [in-flight abort test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon_In-Flight_Abort_Test "Crew Dragon In-Flight Abort Test"), the last test flight before flying crew, in which the Dragon spacecraft fired its launch escape engines in a simulated abort scenario.[\[274\]](#cite_note-274)

On May 30, 2020, the [Crew Dragon Demo-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon_Demo-2 "Crew Dragon Demo-2") mission was launched to the [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station "International Space Station") with NASA astronauts [Bob Behnken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bob_Behnken "Bob Behnken") and [Doug Hurley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doug_Hurley "Doug Hurley"), the first time a crewed vehicle had launched from the U.S. since 2011, and the first SpaceX commercial crewed launch to the ISS.[\[275\]](#cite_note-275) The [Crew-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew-1 "Crew-1") mission was successfully launched to the International Space Station on November 16, 2020, with [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA") astronauts [Michael S. Hopkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_S._Hopkins "Michael S. Hopkins"), [Victor Glover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Glover "Victor Glover") and [Shannon Walker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_Walker "Shannon Walker") along with [JAXA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JAXA "JAXA") astronaut [Soichi Noguchi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soichi_Noguchi "Soichi Noguchi"),[\[276\]](#cite_note-276) all members of the [Expedition 64](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expedition_64 "Expedition 64") crew.[\[277\]](#cite_note-277) On April 23, 2021, [Crew-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew-2 "Crew-2") was launched to the International Space Station with NASA astronauts [Shane Kimbrough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shane_Kimbrough "Shane Kimbrough") and [K. Megan McArthur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K._Megan_McArthur "K. Megan McArthur"), JAXA astronaut [Akihiko Hoshide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akihiko_Hoshide "Akihiko Hoshide"), and [ESA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESA "ESA") astronaut [Thomas Pesquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Pesquet "Thomas Pesquet").[\[278\]](#cite_note-278) The Crew-2 mission successfully docked on April 24, 2021.[\[279\]](#cite_note-279)

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/7/7f/Resilience_after_splashdown.jpg/250px-Resilience_after_splashdown.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:Resilience_after_splashdown.jpg)

_Resilience_ after splashdown

SpaceX also offers paid [crewed spaceflights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crewed_spaceflight "Crewed spaceflight") for private individuals. The first of these missions, [Inspiration4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inspiration4 "Inspiration4"), launched in 2021 on behalf of [Shift4 Payments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shift4_Payments "Shift4 Payments") CEO [Jared Isaacman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jared_Isaacman "Jared Isaacman"). The mission launched the [Crew Dragon _Resilience_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crew_Dragon_Resilience "Crew Dragon Resilience") from the Florida [Kennedy Space Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kennedy_Space_Center "Kennedy Space Center")'s [Launch Complex 39A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_Complex_39A "Launch Complex 39A") atop a [Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_Block_5 "Falcon 9 Block 5") launch vehicle, placed the Dragon [capsule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_capsule "Space capsule") into [low Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit "Low Earth orbit"), and ended successfully about three days later when the _Resilience_ splashed down in the Atlantic Ocean. All four crew members received commercial astronaut training from SpaceX. The training included lessons in orbital mechanics, operating in a microgravity environment, stress testing, emergency-preparedness training, and mission simulations.[\[280\]](#cite_note-280)

### National defense

[![](//thumb.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/df/STP-2_Mission_%2848129211908%29.jpg/250px-STP-2_Mission_%2848129211908%29.jpg?utm_source=en.wikipedia.org&utm_campaign=parser&utm_content=thumbnail)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File:STP-2_Mission_\(48129211908\).jpg)

Launch of the [STP-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STP-2 "STP-2") mission on a Falcon Heavy in June 2019

In 2005, SpaceX announced that it had been awarded an [Indefinite Delivery/Indefinite Quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IDIQ "IDIQ") (IDIQ) contract, allowing the [United States Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Air_Force "United States Air Force") to purchase up to $100 million worth of launches from the company.[\[281\]](#cite_note-281) Three years later, [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA "NASA") announced that it had awarded an IDIQ Launch Services contract to SpaceX for up to $1 billion, depending on the number of missions awarded.[\[282\]](#cite_note-282) In December 2012, SpaceX announced its first two launch contracts with the [United States Department of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense "United States Department of Defense") (DoD). The United States Air Force [Space and Missile Systems Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_and_Missile_Systems_Center "Space and Missile Systems Center") awarded SpaceX two EELV-class missions: [Deep Space Climate Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deep_Space_Climate_Observatory "Deep Space Climate Observatory") (DSCOVR) and [Space Test Program 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Test_Program_2 "Space Test Program 2") (STP-2). DSCOVR was launched on a [Falcon 9](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9 "Falcon 9") launch vehicle in 2015, while STP-2 was launched on a [Falcon Heavy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_Heavy "Falcon Heavy") on June 25, 2019.[\[283\]](#cite_note-283)

The [Falcon 9 v1.1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Falcon_9_v1.1 "Falcon 9 v1.1") was certified for [National Security Space Launch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Security_Space_Launch "National Security Space Launch") (NSSL) in 2015, allowing SpaceX to contract launch services to the Air Force for any payloads classified under national security.[\[180\]](#cite_note-NSSL1-180) This broke the monopoly held since 2006 by [United Launch Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Launch_Alliance "United Launch Alliance") (ULA) over U.S. Air Force launches of classified payloads.[\[284\]](#cite_note-284) In April 2016, the U.S. Air Force awarded the first such national security launch to SpaceX to launch the second [GPS III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS_III "GPS III") satellite for $82.7 million.[\[285\]](#cite_note-285) This was approximately 40% less than the estimated cost for similar previous missions.[\[286\]](#cite_note-286) SpaceX also launched the third GPS III launch on June 20, 2020.[\[287\]](#cite_note-287) In March 2018, SpaceX secured an additional $290 million contract from the U.S. Air Force to launch another three GPS III satellites.[\[288\]](#cite_note-288)

The U.S. [National Reconnaissance Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Reconnaissance_Office "National Reconnaissance Office") (NRO) also purchased launches from SpaceX, with the first taking place on May 1, 2017.[\[289\]](#cite_note-289) In February 2019, SpaceX secured a $297 million contract from the U.S. Air Force to launch another three national security missions, all slated to launch no earlier than FY 2021.[\[290\]](#cite_note-290) In August 2020, the [U.S. Space Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Space_Force "U.S. Space Force") awarded its National Security Space Launch (NSSL) contracts for the following 5–7 years. SpaceX won a contract for $316 million for one launch. In addition, SpaceX will handle 40% of the U.S. military's satellite launch requirements over the period.[\[291\]](#cite_note-291)

SpaceX also designs and launches [custom military satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink#Military_satellites "Starlink") for the [Space Development Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Development_Agency "Space Development Agency") as part of a new missile defense system in low Earth orbit.[\[292\]](#cite_note-292) The constellation would give the United States capabilities to sense, target and potentially intercept nuclear missiles and hypersonic weapons launched from anywhere on Earth.[\[293\]](#cite_note-293) Both China and Russia brought concerns to the United Nations about the program,[\[294\]](#cite_note-294) and various organizations warn it could be destabilizing and trigger an [arms race](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arms_race "Arms race") in space.[\[295\]](#cite_note-295)[\[296\]](#cite_note-296)

In March 2024, Reuters reported that, as part of a $1.8 billion contract signed with the National Reconnaissance Office in 2021, SpaceX is building a network of hundreds of [spy satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spy_satellites "Spy satellites"). This new network, Reuters reported, would be able to operate as a swarm in low orbits.[\[297\]](#cite_note-297)

In December 2024, The Wall Street Journal reported that Musk did not have access to government secrets.[\[298\]](#cite_note-298)

## Launch market competition and pricing pressure

SpaceX's low launch prices, especially for [communications satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Communications_satellite "Communications satellite") flying to [geostationary transfer orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostationary_transfer_orbit "Geostationary transfer orbit") (GTO), have resulted in market pressure on its competitors to lower their own prices.[\[20\]](#cite_note-Changing-20) Prior to 2013, the openly competed comsat launch market had been dominated by Arianespace (flying the [Ariane 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_5 "Ariane 5")) and [International Launch Services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Launch_Services "International Launch Services") (flying the [Proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton_\(rocket_family\) "Proton (rocket family)")).[\[299\]](#cite_note-bbc20131203-299) With a published price of $56.5 million per launch to [low Earth orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_Earth_orbit "Low Earth orbit"), Falcon 9 rockets were the least expensive in the industry.[\[300\]](#cite_note-300) European satellite operators are pushing the ESA to reduce launch prices of the Ariane 5 and [Ariane 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_6 "Ariane 6") rockets as a result of competition from SpaceX.[\[301\]](#cite_note-smh20150519-301)

SpaceX ended the United Launch Alliance (ULA) monopoly of U.S. military payloads when it began to compete for national security launches. In 2015, anticipating a slump in domestic, military, and spy launches, ULA stated that it would go out of business unless it won commercial satellite launch orders.[\[302\]](#cite_note-302) To that end, ULA announced a major restructuring of processes and workforce to decrease launch costs by half.[\[303\]](#cite_note-303)[\[304\]](#cite_note-lat20141212-304)

Congressional testimony by SpaceX in 2017 suggested that the NASA [Space Act Agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Act_Agreement "Space Act Agreement") process of "setting only a high-level requirement for cargo transport to the space station \[while\] leaving the details to industry" had allowed SpaceX to design and develop the Falcon 9 rocket on its own at a substantially lower cost. According to NASA's own independently verified numbers, SpaceX's total development cost for the Falcon 9 rocket, including the Falcon 1 rocket, was estimated at $390 million. In 2011, NASA estimated that it would have cost the agency about $4 billion to develop a rocket like the Falcon 9 booster based upon NASA's traditional contracting processes, about ten times more.[\[258\]](#cite_note-Zapata_2017-258) In May 2020, NASA administrator [Jim Bridenstine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jim_Bridenstine "Jim Bridenstine") remarked that thanks to NASA's investments into SpaceX, the United States has 70% of the commercial launch market, a major improvement since 2012 when there were no commercial launches from the country.[\[305\]](#cite_note-305)

As of 2024, SpaceX operates a Rideshare and Bandwagon (mid inclination) programs. This provides additional competition for small satellite launchers.[\[306\]](#cite_note-306)

### Leadership changes

### Net operating loss carryforwards

As of 2024, SpaceX has likely paid little to no federal income taxes since it was founded; it had a carryforward of $5 billion in [net operating losses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_operating_loss "Net operating loss") that can be used to offset taxes due on its next $5 billion of income.[\[345\]](#cite_note-345)

### Workplace culture

According to former NASA deputy administrator [Lori Garver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lori_Garver "Lori Garver") in 2022, the company overall has a male-dominated "[bro culture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bro_culture "Bro culture")", similar to that of the spaceflight industry in general.[\[346\]](#cite_note-346) In December 2021, claims of workplace sexual harassment from five former SpaceX employees, ranging from interns to full engineers, were published.[\[347\]](#cite_note-fiveformer-347) The former employees claimed to have experienced unwanted advances and uncomfortable interactions.[\[348\]](#cite_note-348) Additionally, the accounts included claims of a culture of [sexual harassment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_harassment "Sexual harassment") existing at the company and one where complaints made to executives, managers, and human resources officers went largely unaddressed.[\[347\]](#cite_note-fiveformer-347)

In May 2022, an anonymous friend of a SpaceX flight attendant alleged that Musk engaged in sexual misconduct with such SpaceX flight attendant in a private jet in 2016 and that the company paid her $250,000 for her silence.[\[349\]](#cite_note-349) In response, some employees collaborated on an open letter condemning "Elon's harmful Twitter behavior".[\[350\]](#cite_note-350) It also asks the company to clearly define SpaceX's "no-asshole" and "[zero tolerance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_tolerance "Zero tolerance")" policies, which it says is unequally enforced from one employee to the next. The next day, Gwynne Shotwell announced that those employees who were involved with the letter had been terminated and claimed that unsponsored, unsolicited surveys were sent to employees during the work day and that some felt pressured to sign the letter.[\[351\]](#cite_note-351)

The company has also been described as having a work culture that pushes employees to work excessively and is described as fostering a [burnout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Occupational_burnout "Occupational burnout") culture.[\[352\]](#cite_note-brutal-352) According to a memo by [Blue Origin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Origin "Blue Origin"), a rival aerospace company with a history of lawsuits and anti-SpaceX political lobbying,[\[353\]](#cite_note-353)[\[354\]](#cite_note-354)[\[355\]](#cite_note-355) SpaceX expected very long work hours, work on weekends, and limited use of holidays.[\[352\]](#cite_note-brutal-352)

In 2023, SpaceX employees alleged that the company's rush to colonize space has resulted in injuries. An examination of OSHA's records revealed injury rates higher than the industry's averages. In addition, in 2023, _[Reuters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reuters "Reuters")_ documented at least 600 previously unreported workplace injuries at SpaceX since 2014, including "crushed limbs, amputations, electrocutions, head and eye wounds and one death", Lonnie LeBlanc, a former United States Marine who was blown off a trailer by the wind while attempting to hold its cargo load down.[\[356\]](#cite_note-356)[\[357\]](#cite_note-357)

In June 2024, eight ex-employees, the same who had previously been fired for penning the open letter against Elon Musk, filed a lawsuit against Musk and SpaceX alleging sexual harassment and discrimination.[\[358\]](#cite_note-358)[\[359\]](#cite_note-359)[\[360\]](#cite_note-360) The lawsuit has since stalled on questions of which court has jurisdiction over SpaceX's headquarters.[\[361\]](#cite_note-361)[\[362\]](#cite_note-362)
